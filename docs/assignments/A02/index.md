# A2 – Truss Stress Analysis

## Objective
- Design a lightweight planar truss using A500 steel or an alternative material.
- Create free body diagrams (FBDs) for joints and critical pins.
- Calculate the required cross-sectional area of truss elements with a safety factor.
- Determine pin sizes based on shear forces with a safety factor.
- Solve equations symbolically and numerically for both truss and pin design.
- Estimate the total weight of the truss and pins.
- Create a CAD model with accurate dimensions and connections.
- Compare CAD weight predictions with hand calculations.
- Document key engineering lessons learned from the process.

### Truss Sketch and Member Values (2a i)
To begin the assignment, I created the geometry of the truss using the formula 2J = M + R, where J = number of joints, M = number of members, and R = number of relative forces. By following the given constraints, I was able to determine the number of relative forces using the knowledge I have about pins and rollers. Attaching this to the previously mentioned formula, we now know that the truss is statically determinate. While solving out the values of the forces, I also went ahead and found the lengths for each of the members for the future.

### Calculating the Cross-sectional Area (2b)
With every internal force now found, we can use the largest one to begin finding the cross-sectional area of the entire truss. The material used to calculate the value was A500 steel (specifically grade C), with the properties listed under the Shaped Structural Tubing Steel section: https://beamdimensions.com/materials/Steel/ASTM/ASTM_A500/. The safety factor used was 3.5. Before beginning the calculations, I listed what information I had and what was needed, along with the equations I needed to find the unknown values. I then used those equations to calculate the minimum cross-sectional area of the truss as well as the overall weight, rounding out the hand calculations for the truss on its own.

## Designing the Pins (3)
Now that our truss is drawn and defined, we need to create pins to hold it all together at the joints. The pins need to be strong enough to handle the shear stress generated by the members when they are attached to the truss, while ensuring they don't add too much weight. To accomplish this, we need to determine the minimum cross-sectional area of the pin while abiding by our safety factor of 4.
